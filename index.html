      The practical guide to chronological age

      How to write it, convert it, use it with norm tables, and work it out in a spreadsheet.

      FormatsConvertingAge bandsRetestsComparing agesSpreadsheetsChecklist

      Chronological age is simply the time since birth. The skill is in writing it the way each form, test or system expects. One child can be "8;7;9", "8:7", "8.61 years" or "103 months" on four different documents, and every one of them is correct. A good chronological age calculator gives you all of them at once, so you never have to convert by hand.

      1. The common formats of chronological age

      Different fields grew their own habits. Here is the same age, 8 years, 7 months and 9 days, written each way:

      FormatExampleWhere you see it
      Years;months;days8;7;9Psychological and speech-language record forms
      Years:months8:7Norm tables and age-equivalent charts
      Years-months8-7School reports and some score summaries
      Words8 years 7 monthsLetters, referrals and parent reports
      Total months103 monthsInfant scales, growth charts, research data
      Decimal years8.61Statistics, growth curves, sports science
      Total days3,145 daysNeonatal care, precise research timing

      Watch out for one trap: 8:7 is not 8.7. The colon means 8 years and 7 months (8.58 years). A decimal point means 8 and seven-tenths of a year (about 8 years 8 months). Mixing them up is one of the most common errors in reports.

      2. Converting between formats

      The calculator does these conversions for you, but it helps to know the rules:

      • To total months: years × 12 + months. 8;7 → 8 × 12 + 7 = 103 months.
      • To decimal years: total days ÷ 365.2425. Using days instead of months keeps the answer accurate to the day.
      • To decimal months: total months + days ÷ 30.4375 (the average month). 103 months and 9 days ≈ 103.3 months.
      • Back from decimal years: multiply the decimal part by 12 for months. 8.61 → 0.61 × 12 = 7.3, so about 8 years 7 months.

      Converting back from a decimal loses a few days, so keep the date of birth on file. You can always rebuild the exact age from it, but not the other way round.

      3. Age bands and norm tables

      Standardised tests don't compare a child with everyone. They compare the child with others in the same age band, a slice of age such as 7;6 to 7;8. Young children change quickly, so their bands are narrow, often one to three months. Teenagers and adults change slowly, so their bands may span a year or more.

      To find the band, take the age in whole months and see which slice it falls into. A child of 7;7;20 is in the 7;6–7;8 band with three-month bands, and in the 7;6–7;11 band with six-month bands. The days don't move the child up a band. Only the month count does, unless your manual tells you to round.

      The age band tab in the calculator shows the current band, the day the child entered it, and the day the next band begins. That is useful when a test date is still flexible. Two weeks either way can decide which norm table applies, and an examiner may prefer to test well inside a band rather than on its edge.

      4. Planning retests and reviews

      Many assessments are repeated to measure progress, and the timing matters. Retest too soon and the scores can rise just because the person remembers the tasks, the so-called practice effect. Leave it too long and you may miss the moment to change support. Test manuals and local guidelines usually suggest a minimum gap. Follow them, and record the chronological age at every test so progress can be read against the right norms.

      In the United States, special-education law (IDEA) requires a re-evaluation at least once every three years unless parents and the school agree it is not needed. Many teams also review goals every year. The retest timeline tool lists the chronological age and band for every date in such a cycle, so you can spot in advance when a child will move into a new band or a new version of a test.

      5. Comparing two ages

      The gap between two people's ages never changes. A brother born 2 years, 3 months and 10 days before his sister stays exactly that much older for life. What does change is the ratio. When he is 4 and she is not yet 2, he is more than twice her age. When he is 40 and she is 37, he is only about 6% older. That is why a one-year gap feels huge in a nursery and invisible at work.

      The compare tool also answers two questions people often ask: when was the older person the age the younger one is now? and when will the younger person reach the older one's current age? Families use it for fun. Coaches use the same idea when two players sit on either side of an age-group cut-off.

      6. Calculating chronological age in Excel and Google Sheets

      Spreadsheets are handy for a caseload or a class. Put the date of birth in A2 and the test date in B2:

      Years=DATEDIF(A2, B2, "y")

      Months=DATEDIF(A2, B2, "ym")

      Total months=DATEDIF(A2, B2, "m")

      Decimal years=(B2-A2)/365.2425

      For the days, avoid DATEDIF(A2,B2,"md"). Microsoft's own help page warns that this unit can return wrong or negative results around the end of a month. A safer formula counts the days from the last month-anniversary: =B2-EDATE(A2, DATEDIF(A2,B2,"m")). Then check a few rows against this calculator, especially birthdays on the 29th, 30th or 31st.

      7. Checklist before you report a chronological age

      1. Is the date of birth taken from an official record, not typed from memory?
      2. Is the "age on" date the day of testing, or of the event the rule refers to?
      3. Is the format the one the form or norm table expects (; or : or decimal)?
      4. Has rounding been applied only if the manual says so?
      5. For a baby born early, does the report also need corrected age?
      6. Does the age band match the norm table you used to score?

      Questions, answered

      01Is chronological age the same as age?

      Yes, it is your age counted from your date of birth. The word "chronological" is used when the exact figure in years, months and days is needed, or to separate it from other measures such as mental, developmental or biological age.

      02How do I convert chronological age to decimal years?

      Divide the number of days between the date of birth and the date by 365.2425, the average length of a calendar year. For example, 3,145 days is 8.61 years. The Formats tab does this for you.

      03How do I calculate chronological age in Excel?

      Use DATEDIF(birth, date, "y") for years and DATEDIF(birth, date, "ym") for the extra months. For days, subtract the date of the last month-anniversary from the date instead of relying on the "md" unit, which Microsoft warns can give wrong results.

      04What is an age band?

      An age band is a range of ages, such as 7;6 to 7;8, that shares one norm table in a standardised test. Bands are often 1, 2, 3, 4, 6 or 12 months wide, depending on the test and the age group.

      05When does a child move into the next age band?

      On the day their age in whole months reaches the first month of the next band. The age band finder shows that date and how many days are left.

      06How old was I on a past date?

      Enter your date of birth and set "Age on" to the past date. The calculator gives your exact age on that day.

      07How do I compare two people's ages?

      Use the compare tool. It shows who is older, the exact gap, when the older person was the younger person's current age, and when the younger person will reach the older person's current age.

      08Does the calculator work for adults and older people?

      Yes. Any date of birth from 1900 onwards works, and the same calendar rules apply at every age.
